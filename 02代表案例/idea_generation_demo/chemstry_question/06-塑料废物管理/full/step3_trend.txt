Trends:  
Paper 0 to Paper 1: Paper 0, “Detecting Contaminants in Post-Consumer Plastic Packaging Waste by a NIR Hyperspectral Imaging-Based Cascade Detection Approach” (2021), treats plastic-waste management primarily as a sorting-quality problem: post-consumer packaging must be cleaned of impurities before mechanical recycling can be effective. Its main contribution is the use of near-infrared hyperspectral imaging in a cascade-detection framework to identify contaminants inside plastic packaging waste. Paper 1, “Identification of Aged Polypropylene with Machine Learning and Near-Infrared Spectroscopy for Improved Recycling” (2025), builds on the same broad premise—optical sensing can make mixed plastic waste legible—but shifts the target from contaminant detection to material-state recognition. Instead of asking only “Is there an impurity?”, it asks “Can we identify aged polypropylene well enough to improve sorting?” This is an important advance because polymer aging, surface condition, and material history can affect recyclability, meaning that successful recycling depends not only on separating foreign objects but also on distinguishing usable from degraded plastic. The transition therefore moves from impurity screening toward quality-aware plastic classification.

Paper 1 to Paper 2: Paper 2, “Development of an Economically Viable AI-Enabled Sorting Technology for Complex Plastic Waste Streams” (2025), expands the scope again. Where Paper 1 focuses on a specific material class—aged polypropylene—using machine learning and near-infrared spectroscopy, Paper 2 frames the challenge as a system-level sorting problem for complex plastic streams, especially difficult categories such as #3–#7 plastics. Its advance is the integration of artificial intelligence with sensor fusion and an explicit emphasis on economic viability. This matters because real recycling facilities do not only need accurate laboratory-style classification; they need technologies that can operate on heterogeneous, high-throughput waste streams at a cost that facilities can bear. The progression therefore moves from single-purpose contaminant detection, to targeted polymer-condition identification, to a broader AI-enabled sorting architecture designed for industrially complex waste.

Key Turning Points:  
The first turning point is the move from manual or coarse mechanical sorting toward sensor-based characterization. The 2021 paper shows that plastic-waste management can be improved by making contaminants visible through hyperspectral imaging, thereby addressing a core weakness in conventional recycling chains: the inability to systematically identify impurities.  

The second turning point is the recognition that polymer identity alone is insufficient. The 2025 aged-polypropylene paper introduces the idea that material condition—especially aging—must be detected if recycling is to avoid downcycling, mis-sorting, or poor-quality output. This shifts the research emphasis from “what polymer is present?” to “is this polymer still suitable for recycling?”  

The third turning point is the shift from single-sensor analytics to integrated, economically constrained sorting systems. The second 2025 paper treats plastic waste management as an engineering and economic system problem, combining AI with multiple sensing modalities to address hard-to-sort plastics. This marks a conceptual move from analytical detection toward deployable sorting infrastructure.  

Although the graph does not contain explicit predecessor or parallel-work edges, these three papers reveal a clear thematic trajectory: spectroscopy-based detection, machine-learning-based material-state recognition, and AI-enabled sensor-fusion sorting.

Recurring Limitations:  
A recurring limitation across the papers is the difficulty of handling heterogeneous, mixed, contaminated, or aged plastic waste. The 2021 paper emphasizes impurities in post-consumer packaging; the first 2025 paper emphasizes the inefficiency of manual sorting and the challenge of mixed plastics; the second 2025 paper emphasizes the difficulty of sorting complex #3–#7 streams. All three imply that simple, single-step sorting is inadequate for real-world plastic waste.  

Another recurring limitation is the dependence on optical or spectroscopic signals in conditions that are likely to be noisy and variable. Surface contamination, additives, pigments, aging, moisture, multilayer structures, and mixed polymer compositions can all degrade classification performance. The papers advance sensing methods, but they do not fully resolve the scientific problem of robust recognition under uncontrolled, dirty, industrial conditions.  

A third recurring limitation is scalability. The 2021 paper focuses on detection, the 2025 polypropylene paper on improved identification, and the 2025 sensor-fusion paper on economically viable sorting. However, the broader challenge of translating accurate characterization into reliable, low-cost, high-throughput recycling operations remains only partially solved.  

Structural Gaps (open SCIENTIFIC problems in the field that remain unsolved — NOT missing papers, absent milestones, or graph-wiring issues):  
One open scientific problem is robust online characterization of dirty, aged, contaminated, pigmented, multilayer, or additive-laden plastics. Existing NIR-based approaches are promising, but real waste streams contain surface degradation, food residue, labels, inks, fillers, and polymer blends that can obscure or distort spectral signatures. A unresolved challenge is to develop sensing and modeling methods that remain accurate under these conditions without requiring unrealistic sample preparation.  

A second open problem is generalization across waste streams, facilities, instruments, and geographic contexts. Machine-learning models trained on one type of plastic waste may not transfer well to another because composition, contamination levels, aging patterns, and sensor calibration differ. The field still needs scientifically grounded methods for domain adaptation, calibration transfer, uncertainty quantification, and confidence-aware decision-making in sorting systems.  

A third open problem is optimal sensor fusion for plastic waste. Paper 2 points toward sensor fusion, but the scientific question remains: which combinations of spectral, visual, chemical, thermal, density-based, or mechanical signals are most informative for different plastic categories, and how should their outputs be fused under cost, speed, and hardware constraints? This is not merely an engineering issue; it requires understanding which physical properties distinguish recyclable, degraded, contaminated, or hazardous plastics.  

A fourth open problem is linking real-time material characterization to circular-economy decision-making. The broader topic emphasizes the need for better data on plastic-waste flows, but the papers in the graph focus mainly on detection and sorting. A major unresolved scientific challenge is how to connect sensor-level classifications with material flow analysis, recycling yield, economic feasibility, environmental impact, and policy interventions such as bottle recycling, bag bans, or waste-to-fuel use.  

A fifth open problem is the management of difficult plastic categories, especially #3–#7 plastics, multilayer packaging, and mixed polymers. These materials are often hard to identify, separate, and recycle because their composition is heterogeneous and their economic value is low. The field has not yet solved how to combine sensing, artificial intelligence, and physical or chemical separation mechanisms in a way that makes these streams reliably recoverable at scale.
