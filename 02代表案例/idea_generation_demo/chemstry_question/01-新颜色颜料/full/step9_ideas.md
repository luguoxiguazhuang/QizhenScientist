# Latent Defect-Optical Surrogates for Discovering High-NIR Red Mn/Cr Oxide Pigments

## Motivation

The original query asks whether more color pigments can be discovered. The answer is yes if new pigment materials, not new perceptual color categories, are found by design. Red Mn-oxide and Cr-doped oxide systems already show that visible red color can be coupled to near-infrared reflectance [Gramm et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/zaac.202000274) [Divya et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ceramint.2021.07.218) [Liu et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ceramint.2025.08.005), and the YInMn-blue case shows that unusual local coordination can create intense pigment color [Ransmayr et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevmaterials.6.105003). The remaining bottleneck is predictive: pigment discovery is still empirical because local coordination, defect states, electronic transitions, powder scattering, and NIR loss are not jointly modeled. This idea transfers surrogate hidden representations from data-efficient predictive chemistry [Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1039/d5dd00256g) and physics-informed machine learning for chemical systems [Keith et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.chemrev.1c00107) to pigment science. Instead of a generic defect similarity score, the method learns a hidden latent representation from physically assignable defect-loss optical channels, enabling controlled discovery of new high-NIR red pigment variants.

## Method

This method is intended to solve the lack of a predictive, defect-specific route to new high-NIR red oxide pigments; it can be divided into 4 parts: (1) a fixed-hue, morphology-controlled defect library, (2) decomposition of optical loss into physically assignable defect channels, (3) a latent defect-optical surrogate whose hidden representation is trained on those channels, and (4) a mechanistic applicability domain for selecting new pigment variants. The primary testable hypothesis is that a hidden representation trained to reconstruct defect-specific loss channels predicts fixed-hue NIR reflectance changes caused by controlled defect populations more accurately than composition-only or explicit-descriptor models, and that this improvement is not explained by particle scattering, phase impurity, or composition alone.

**1. Fixed-hue defect library with morphology and phase controls**

Use Li2MnO3 as the primary red host because it is a rare Mn4+ red oxide and a NIR-reflective pigment target [Gramm et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/zaac.202000274). Prepare one phase-pure batch, then split it into aliquots and anneal at the same temperature and time under different oxygen partial pressures. This varies only the oxygen-vacancy and Mn3+/Mn4+ defect population. Thermal treatment is known to alter local transition-metal environments and color in Mn-doped oxides [Oshita et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.35848/1347-4065/ad17e0). Particle morphology and powder scattering are controlled by milling, sieving, and imaging, because pigment color depends on morphology and scattering [Splinter et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules29153527) [Ransmayr et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevmaterials.6.105003). CIELAB hue angle and color contrast are used to define a fixed-hue acceptance window [Ferro et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.omx.2022.100167).

**2. Physically assignable defect-loss decomposition**

Convert diffuse reflectance to Kubelka-Munk absorption and fit the visible-NIR spectrum as a sum of mechanistic loss channels: Mn4+ d-d transitions, Mn3+/Mn4+ intervalence charge transfer, oxygen-vacancy polaron absorption, and a free-carrier or broad NIR tail; for Cr-containing candidates add Cr3+ site-distortion transitions [Ransmayr et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevmaterials.6.105003) [Liu et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ceramint.2025.08.005). The spectral fit is constrained by XPS, XAS, and EPR quantification of Mn valence and defect proxies, so the channels represent causal defect states rather than generic descriptors. This decomposition directly addresses the need to move from broad defect similarity to assignable loss mechanisms in red NIR pigments [Gramm et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/zaac.202000274).

**3. Latent defect-optical surrogate**

Borrow the surrogate hidden representation mechanism from data-efficient predictive chemistry [Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1039/d5dd00256g). Train a surrogate model first to predict the defect-loss channel vector from physics-informed inputs, then use its hidden layer as a latent defect-optical representation for downstream prediction of fixed-hue NIR reflectance and hue stability. Inputs include composition, oxidation state, polyhedral distortion, bond distance, vacancy formation propensity, and transition energy, following hybrid machine-learning and computational-chemistry practice [Keith et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.chemrev.1c00107). Baselines and controls include composition-only, explicit-descriptor-only, defect-channel-only linear models, unstructured feature concatenation, and randomized latent features or shuffled channel labels, with model capacity and data splits held fixed.

**4. Mechanistic applicability domain and discovery loop**

Define an applicability domain using polyhedral distortion, vacancy formation propensity, transition-energy overlap, and defect-loss channel similarity, not merely composition distance. Screen known red and orange Mn/Cr oxide hosts and variants, including Li3AlMnO5 [Divya et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ceramint.2021.07.218), La3LiMnO7 [Oka et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules26206243), and Cr-doped LaAlO3 [Liu et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ceramint.2025.08.005). Select high-ranking in-domain candidates and matched out-of-domain or random candidates as controls. Synthesize and measure all candidates with the same powder standard to test whether the latent defect-optical model discovers new high-NIR pigment variants rather than merely fitting known compositions.
