Trends:

Paper 0 to Paper 1:  
Using the temporal order implied by publication years, Paper 0 is the 2020 work on the Soai reaction, *“Structural Contributions to Autocatalysis and Asymmetric Amplification in the Soai Reaction.”* This paper treats chirality primarily as a mechanistic chemical problem: how a small enantiomeric imbalance can be amplified through autocatalysis, mixed catalyst–substrate states, and structural feedback in a specific organic reaction. Its central contribution is to show that strong asymmetric amplification is chemically possible, but it remains tied to a specialized reaction system. Paper 1, the 2021 work *“Emergence of Homochirality in Large Molecular Systems,”* moves the question from a single reaction to system-level chiral symmetry breaking. It invokes Frank-type nonequilibrium network models and addresses their limitations when applied to large molecular systems. The transition is therefore from “how can one reaction amplify chirality?” to “how can many interacting molecular components collectively select one handedness?” This is a key conceptual advance because biological homochirality is not a property of one molecule but of coupled biochemical networks.

Paper 1 to Paper 2:  
Paper 2, the 2023 work *“Origin of Homochirality: The Formation and Stability of Homochiral Peptides in Aqueous Prebiological Environment in the Earth’s Crust,”* shifts the emphasis from abstract network models to geochemically plausible origin-of-life settings. It asks whether homochiral peptides could form and remain stable in aqueous crustal or hydrothermal environments, where flow, dilution, racemization, and chemical complexity could oppose chiral selection. The advance is environmental and biochemical realism: rather than asking only whether homochirality can emerge in principle, it asks whether homochiral molecular assemblies could persist under conditions relevant to early Earth. This moves the field closer to the biological question by connecting chiral emergence to peptides, prebiotic self-assembly, and habitats associated with early life.

Key Turning Points:

1. From asymmetric amplification to network-level homochirality.  
   The Soai reaction demonstrates that autocatalytic chemistry can amplify chirality, but the 2021 paper marks a turn toward explaining how such amplification can operate across large molecular systems. This is a shift from isolated chemical mechanism to collective symmetry breaking.

2. From idealized Frank-type models to realistic large-system constraints.  
   Frank’s classic model is treated as a foundational idea, but its limitations become the focus of later work. The field turns toward questions of robustness: can homochirality emerge when many species, side reactions, finite-size effects, stochastic fluctuations, or molecular complexity are present?

3. From abstract emergence to environmental feasibility.  
   The 2023 paper marks a turn toward geological and prebiotic plausibility. The central issue becomes not merely whether homochirality can arise mathematically or in specialized chemistry, but whether it can arise and persist in environments compatible with early life.

4. From “how did homochirality arise?” to “why does life require it?”  
   The seed topic, especially the focus on electron transfer in protein assemblies, points to a functional turn: homochirality may matter because it enables efficient molecular recognition, folding, catalysis, and bioenergetic processes. However, the cited subgraph mainly addresses emergence rather than functional necessity, leaving an implicit turning point between origin and biological requirement.

Recurring Limitations:

1. The initiation problem remains unresolved.  
   The papers address amplification or emergence after some initial chiral bias, but the transition from a racemic or nearly racemic prebiotic mixture to a decisively homochiral system remains difficult to explain without special assumptions.

2. Environmental realism is still incomplete.  
   Models of chiral symmetry breaking often assume controlled nonequilibrium conditions, while prebiotic environments involve flow, dilution, mineral surfaces, temperature gradients, competing reactions, and racemization. The 2023 paper moves toward this realism but does not fully close the gap between model chemistry and messy early-Earth conditions.

3. The link between emergence and biological function is weak.  
   The subgraph explains how homochirality might arise, but it does not fully demonstrate why homochirality became obligatory for life. The functional advantage of homochirality—such as improved electron transfer, replication fidelity, catalytic specificity, or molecular recognition—is not quantitatively established across the papers.

4. Chemical scope is narrow.  
   The Soai reaction is powerful but chemically special. Peptide-focused work addresses one class of biomolecules. Neither fully explains the coordinated homochirality of amino acids, sugars, nucleic acids, and lipids in living systems.

5. There is no general metric for “life’s requirement” for chirality.  
   The papers do not provide a framework for deciding whether chirality is a universal requirement for life, a contingent feature of terrestrial life, or a context-dependent advantage that could be replaced in alternative biochemistries.

Structural Gaps (open SCIENTIFIC problems in the field that remain unsolved — NOT missing papers, absent milestones, or graph-wiring issues):

1. The necessity-versus-contingency problem.  
   It remains unresolved whether homochirality is a universal requirement for life or a frozen accident of terrestrial biochemistry. A major open problem is to determine whether alternative life-like systems could function with racemic mixtures, mixed handedness, or opposite chirality while still supporting replication, metabolism, and evolution.

2. The functional advantage of homochirality is not quantitatively explained.  
   Although chirality is known to affect molecular recognition, folding, and reaction specificity, there is no complete physical-chemical theory showing why homochirality confers a decisive advantage in proto-living or living systems. In particular, the relationship between homochirality and electron transfer, catalytic efficiency, error suppression, or information fidelity remains underdeveloped.

3. No integrated route connects chiral amplification to protocellular life.  
   A persistent scientific gap is the absence of a unified pathway that links asymmetric autocatalysis, network-level symmetry breaking, environmental selection, polymer formation, compartmentalization, and heredity. Current approaches tend to address only one or two of these stages.

4. The selection pressure for homochirality before life is unclear.  
   It is not yet known what prebiotic selective force, if any, made homochirality advantageous before Darwinian evolution began. Candidate advantages include greater polymer stability, improved catalysis, reduced cross-inhibition, better self-assembly, or more efficient energy transfer, but none has been established as a general mechanism.

5. Cross-polymer chiral coordination remains unexplained.  
   Life uses homochiral amino acids, sugars, nucleic acids, and related cofactors in a mutually compatible way. An unresolved problem is how separate chiral selections became coupled into a single, coherent biochemical system rather than remaining isolated chiral preferences.

6. The relationship between chirality and alternative definitions of life is undeveloped.  
   If life is defined by metabolism, information, evolution, compartmentalization, or thermodynamic persistence, the role of chirality may differ. There is no general framework that predicts when chirality becomes necessary under different definitions of life or in non-terrestrial biochemical contexts.
