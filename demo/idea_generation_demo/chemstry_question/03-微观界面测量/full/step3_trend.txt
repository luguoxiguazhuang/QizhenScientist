Key Turning Points:  
1. From single-parameter thickness measurement to multimodal in situ characterization: The combination of neutron reflectivity, infrared spectroscopy, and ellipsometry marks a turning point where the field begins to overcome the limitation of optical interference methods that provide only film thickness. The important change is the simultaneous acquisition of structural, optical, and molecular information at solid/liquid interfaces.  
2. From binary interfaces to reactive triple-phase systems: The gas–liquid–solid triple-phase catalysis work expands the scientific scope from relatively simpler liquid/solid or gas/liquid boundaries to technologically important catalytic microenvironments. This is a turning point because wettability and mass transfer become central variables controlling chemical transformation.  
3. From ensemble structural descriptors to nanoscale functional mapping: The 2023 in situ conductive AFM work marks a shift toward directly mapping local electronic and chemical heterogeneity at operating interfaces. This is especially important for electrocatalysis, where local active-site variation can dominate macroscopic performance.  
4. From ex situ or static observation to operando interfacial science: Across the papers, the field increasingly emphasizes measurements under realistic conditions. The common direction is to observe interfaces while electrochemical, catalytic, or transport processes are occurring, rather than reconstructing interfacial behavior after the fact.

Recurring Limitations:  
1. Partial observables from any single technique: Neutron reflectivity and ellipsometry provide powerful structural and thickness information, but they do not directly resolve local electron-transfer heterogeneity. AFM provides nanoscale surface sensitivity and local electrical information, but it cannot easily capture bulk liquid structure, buried interfaces, or long-range mass-transfer fields. Triple-phase wettability/mass-transfer characterization captures functional transport context, but it may lack nanoscale mechanistic resolution.  
2. Difficulty accessing buried and dynamic interfaces: Gas–liquid, liquid–solid, and gas–liquid–solid interfaces are often optically complex, geometrically confined, or chemically reactive. Measuring them without perturbing the very phenomena of interest remains difficult.  
3. Trade-off between spatial resolution, temporal resolution, and chemical specificity: High spatial resolution methods such as AFM may not capture fast molecular rearrangements or large-area transport events, while spectroscopic and reflectivity methods may average over heterogeneous nanoscale domains.  
4. Weak link between local measurements and macroscopic rates: Even when local structure or local conductivity is measured, it remains challenging to quantitatively connect those observations to evaporation rates, heat transfer coefficients, reaction turnover, bubble formation, or mass flux.  
5. Heterogeneity is recognized but not fully resolved: The papers implicitly or explicitly acknowledge that interfaces are spatially heterogeneous, but no method in the graph fully resolves how this heterogeneity controls interfacial chemistry across all relevant length and time scales.

Structural Gaps (open SCIENTIFIC problems in the field that remain unsolved — NOT missing papers, absent milestones, or graph-wiring issues):  
1. No general method exists for simultaneous nanoscale mapping of structure, chemistry, charge transfer, flow, temperature, and mass flux at operating gas–liquid–solid interfaces. Current approaches measure only selected parts of this coupled problem.  
2. The mechanistic connection between nanoscale interfacial heterogeneity and macroscopic transport/reaction rates remains unresolved. For example, local wetting patches, electron-transfer hot spots, adsorbate domains, or nanobubbles are known to matter, but quantitative models linking them to overall evaporation, catalysis, combustion, or cooling performance are still incomplete.  
3. Transient microscopic events such as film rupture, bubble nucleation, local evaporation bursts, microscale explosions, and rapid interfacial reactions are difficult to observe with sufficient spatiotemporal resolution and chemical specificity. Existing methods tend to sacrifice one dimension of resolution for another.  
4. Buried solid–solid and solid–liquid interfaces remain especially challenging. Many high-resolution surface probes are sensitive to exposed surfaces but cannot non-destructively access chemically active buried interfaces under realistic pressure, temperature, or electrochemical conditions.  
5. There is no robust framework for converting multimodal interfacial signals into absolute local fluxes or reaction rates. Neutron reflectivity, ellipsometry, infrared spectroscopy, AFM, and wettability measurements each provide valuable descriptors, but their quantitative integration into a unified interfacial transport model remains an open scientific problem.  
6. Directional interfacial flow and local concentration gradients near microscopic interfaces are still difficult to measure directly. This is particularly important for evaporative cooling, spray drying, combustion, and materials synthesis, where local flow patterns can determine heat and mass transfer.  
7. The field lacks a general strategy for correlating local nanoscale measurements with device-scale performance. Even when nanoscale phenomena are observed, scaling those observations to predict macroscopic behavior in reactors, catalysts, coatings, or thermal-management devices remains unresolved.
