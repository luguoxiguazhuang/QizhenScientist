Trends:  
Paper 0 to Paper 1: Paper 0 is the 2020 study of entrance-channel-dependent hot fusion reactions for superheavy-element synthesis. It treats the search for elements beyond Z = 118 as a reaction-optimization problem: by surveying thousands of projectile–target combinations, it evaluates compound-nucleus formation probability, survival probability, evaporation-residue cross sections, quasifission effects, and entrance-channel asymmetries for Z = 110–126. Its central claim is that carefully chosen hot-fusion routes could produce superheavy nuclei with higher cross sections than many previously measured cases, potentially opening access to the eighth row of the periodic table. Paper 1, the 2025 “Superheavy Nuclei XVII: 1640 ≤ A < 1650 Systems” work, moves the question from “which reactions might synthesize new nuclei?” to “which nuclear interactions and models can credibly describe such extreme systems?” It emphasizes that predictions in the superheavy mass region depend on extrapolating nuclear interactions from better-known nuclei. The transition is therefore from practical synthesis feasibility to theoretical reliability: Paper 0 identifies candidate pathways to new elements, while Paper 1 asks whether the underlying nuclear-structure framework is sufficiently robust to predict stability, decay behavior, and interpretability for those candidates.

Paper 1 to Paper 2: Paper 2, the 2025 simulation study of atmospheric-pressure ion transfer in a gas-flow device connected to the UniCell buffer gas cell, shifts the emphasis from nuclear production and stability to experimental handling and chemical identification. It addresses a major bottleneck for elements beyond Fl/Z = 114: superheavy atoms are produced as rare energetic recoils, often with very short half-lives and low yields, and must be thermalized, transported, and chemically characterized rapidly. The advance is not another fusion reaction calculation but an infrastructure-level response: modeling gas-flow and ion-transfer conditions that could preserve and deliver single atoms for chemical study. This transition shows the field moving from “can we make and predict the nucleus?” to “can we capture, transport, and chemically identify it before it decays?” The two 2025 papers are best viewed as parallel developments: one strengthens the theoretical description of superheavy stability, while the other improves the experimental apparatus needed to verify and chemically place new elements.

Key Turning Points:  
- The IUPAC/IUPAP seed report establishes the conceptual turning point: element discovery is no longer only a matter of observing decay chains, but increasingly depends on new synthesis methods, detection regimes, and criteria for verification. It frames the central question of whether the periodic table is limited by nuclear physics, experimental capability, or discovery standards.  
- The 2020 hot-fusion study marks a methodological turning point: the search for Z > 118 becomes systematically predictive. Instead of relying mainly on past reaction choices, it surveys entrance-channel effects and proposes specific reactions that could yield measurable cross sections, including routes toward very heavy Z = 126 systems.  
- The 2025 superheavy-nuclei theory work marks a second turning point: it makes explicit that synthesis predictions alone are insufficient. The field must confront the model dependence of nuclear interactions, mass predictions, fission barriers, and stability estimates in a region where empirical anchors are sparse.  
- The 2025 gas-flow/chemistry simulation marks a third turning point: identification of future elements is recognized as a time-critical chemical and instrumental problem. For the heaviest elements, discovery may require not only nuclear synthesis but also rapid atom-by-atom chemical handling and element-specific characterization.

Recurring Limitations:  
- Extremely low production probabilities persist. Even optimistic predicted evaporation-residue cross sections remain small, and measured superheavy synthesis often occurs at picobarn-level or lower yields.  
- Quasifission and entrance-channel effects remain unresolved obstacles. Mass asymmetry, charge asymmetry, isospin effects, and non-compound fission can suppress the formation of a fully equilibrated compound nucleus.  
- Theoretical extrapolation is a recurring weakness. Nuclear interactions and stability models must be extended into regions with limited experimental data, creating uncertainty in predicted lifetimes, decay modes, fission barriers, and island-of-stability locations.  
- Short half-lives and low event rates limit chemical and physical characterization. Beyond Fl, atoms may decay before conventional chemical separation or spectroscopy can be completed.  
- Discovery criteria become harder to satisfy when only a few atoms are produced. Unambiguous assignment of atomic number requires correlated decay evidence, chemical behavior, or atomic spectroscopy, but each becomes more difficult as yields fall and lifetimes shorten.

Structural Gaps (open SCIENTIFIC problems in the field that remain unsolved — NOT missing papers, absent milestones, or graph-wiring issues):  
- A unified predictive framework for superheavy-element formation is still lacking. The field needs a model that simultaneously treats entrance-channel dynamics, quasifission, compound-nucleus formation, shell stabilization, fission competition, survival probability, and decay-chain observables with quantified uncertainties.  
- Nuclear interactions in the superheavy region remain insufficiently constrained by experiment. A key open problem is identifying measurable observables—masses, spectroscopy, radii, fission properties, or chemical signatures—that can calibrate models near the limits of the periodic table.  
- There is no fully demonstrated pathway from high-yield synthesis to rapid single-atom chemical identification for Z > 118. Future discovery may require coupling optimized fusion reactions, gas stopping, fast chemical separation, and element-specific detection such as decay correlation, X-ray emission, or atomic spectroscopy in one integrated system.  
- Current synthesis strategies may favor nuclei that are too short-lived for chemical verification. A major unresolved problem is how to produce longer-lived isotopes closer to the island of stability, where chemical properties can be measured and periodic-table placement can be confirmed.  
- The field has not settled what “completion” of the periodic table would mean scientifically. The limit could be set by nuclear instability, by vanishing production cross sections, by the inability to chemically identify single atoms, or by the failure of periodic chemical trends in relativistic superheavy systems. These boundaries are not yet distinguished in a predictive way.  
- Discovery standards for future elements remain scientifically challenging. When only a few atoms exist, the field needs robust statistical and epistemic criteria for assigning atomic number, especially when evidence is indirect and may involve competing interpretations of decay chains or chemical behavior.
