Recurring Limitations:  
No recurring limitations can be observed across multiple papers in the supplied graph because the graph contains no cited papers. At the level of the research topic itself, several persistent scientific limitations are implicit:

1. Human color perception constrains “newness.” New pigments can provide new spectral reflectance curves, chemical compositions, or material properties, but they do not necessarily create fundamentally new perceived color categories.

2. Discovery remains difficult to predict. Historically important pigment discoveries often arose through empirical synthesis or accident, indicating that the field lacks a fully predictive framework connecting composition and structure to stable, useful color.

3. Stability and usability are hard to combine. A candidate pigment must be chemically stable, thermally stable, non-toxic, durable, synthesizable, and economically viable. Many materials may have interesting optical properties but fail one or more of these requirements.

4. The space of inorganic compounds is vast, but the subset that yields safe, stable, intensely colored, practically usable pigments is narrow. This creates a persistent gap between theoretical possibility and practical pigment discovery.

Structural Gaps:  
The following are open scientific problems in the field, not graph-wiring issues or missing-paper issues:

1. Predictive composition-to-color modeling: A major unsolved problem is the ability to predict perceived color from chemical composition, crystal structure, electronic band structure, defect chemistry, and particle morphology. Current understanding can explain many known pigments after the fact, but it does not yet reliably identify which unmade compounds will become useful pigments.

2. Inverse design of pigments: The field lacks a robust inverse-design framework in which a desired optical response, hue, saturation, brightness, durability, and toxicity profile can be specified first, and then candidate materials can be computationally generated and experimentally tested.

3. Stable versus metastable pigment discovery: Many potentially useful pigments may exist only as metastable phases or may require narrow synthesis conditions. A scientific gap remains in predicting which metastable compounds can be synthesized, isolated, and retained under practical conditions.

4. Linking spectral novelty to perceptual relevance: Because new pigments do not necessarily create new colors in a perceptual sense, an open problem is to quantify when a new spectral reflectance curve is meaningfully novel to human vision, especially in terms of gamut expansion, metamerism, saturation, and viewing-condition robustness.

5. Simultaneous optimization of color, safety, and durability: There is no general scientific framework that efficiently searches for materials that are simultaneously strongly colored, non-toxic, earth-abundant, chemically inert, lightfast, thermally stable, and scalable to manufacture.

6. High-throughput and autonomous pigment discovery: While computational screening and high-throughput synthesis are advancing, a closed-loop system that can propose, synthesize, characterize, and validate new pigment candidates with minimal human intervention remains an open challenge.

7. Discovery beyond known pigment families: Much modern pigment chemistry is concentrated in known structural families such as oxides, sulfides, silicates, and related inorganic systems. A scientific gap is whether entirely new structural or bonding motifs can yield stable, practical pigments with unusual optical properties.

8. Functional color materials: Another open problem is designing pigments whose color is coupled to additional functions, such as infrared reflectance, heat management, environmental sensing, or reversible color change, without compromising stability or visual performance.
